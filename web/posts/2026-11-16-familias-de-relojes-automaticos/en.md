There is no official way to classify watches. But any collector knows what a diver, a pilot's watch or a dress watch is. Almost all of these **families** began as tools for a specific job in the 20th century, and today they mostly help with two things: choosing what to wear and seeing what kind of watch your collection is missing.

There are two useful ways to classify: by **function**, which is the family, and by **form**, which is the case. Complications such as the date or moon phase are a separate subject, covered in [this article](../automatic-watch-complications/).

## The families at a glance

| Family | Where it comes from | How to recognise it |
|---|---|---|
| Diver | Professional and military diving, 1950s | Rotating bezel, plenty of lume, 100 m or more |
| Chronograph | Racing, aviation, the lab | Two pushers and sub-dials |
| GMT | Commercial aviation, 1950s | A fourth, 24-hour hand |
| Pilot | Military aviation, 1930s and 40s | Highly legible black dial, triangle at 12, large crown |
| Field | Land armies, 1940s | Simple, tough, Arabic numerals |
| Dress | Shirts and suits | Thin, clean dial, leather strap |
| Sport | The 1970s | Bracelet integrated into the case |
| GADA | Nothing in particular | Good for everything |

## Diver

The modern dive watch was born in the 1950s. In 1953 Blancpain introduced the Fifty Fathoms, one of the first. Since 1996 there has been a standard, **ISO 6425**, setting the minimum a watch must meet to be called a diver:

- Water resistance of at least **100 metres**.
- A way to time a dive, usually a **rotating bezel**. A unidirectional bezel is recommended: if it gets knocked, it shows less time than has passed, never more.
- The time must be readable **at 25 cm in total darkness**.

Plenty of diver-style watches are not certified, and you don't need certification for the pool or the beach.

## Chronograph

You can spot one by the two pushers beside the crown and the small sub-dials that count the timed minutes and hours. Many also have a tachymeter scale on the bezel for working out speeds. Its story as an automatic begins in 1969, and we tell it in the [complications article](../automatic-watch-complications/).

## GMT

A GMT has a fourth hand that goes round once every 24 hours and shows a second time zone, often against a two-colour 24-hour bezel for day and night. The model that set the style appeared in 1954, developed for Pan Am pilots who crossed the Atlantic and needed both home and destination time.

## Pilot

The most copied pilot's watch is the German **B-Uhr**, ordered by the Luftwaffe from 1935. It measured about 55 mm so it could be read at a glance in a cockpit, and only five houses made it: A. Lange & Söhne, Laco, Stowa, Wempe and IWC. There were two dials:

- **Type A:** a single hour scale, with a triangle and two dots at 12.
- **Type B,** from 1941: minutes on the outer edge and hours on a smaller inner circle.

That is where almost every modern pilot's watch gets its traits: black dial, big white numerals, a triangle at 12 for orientation and a large crown you can turn with gloves on.

## Field

The field watch is the land version: simple, legible and tough. The classic example is the set of watches the British Ministry of Defence ordered from twelve brands in 1945, including Longines, Omega and IWC, which collectors call the *Dirty Dozen*. Black dial, luminous Arabic numerals, small seconds and a waterproof case. Field watches tend to be modestly sized and worn on fabric or leather straps.

## Dress

A dress watch is thin, understated and easy to read: clean dial, slim markers, leather strap and a case that slips under a shirt cuff. Water resistance and lume don't matter here. Thickness does.

## Sport

This is the youngest family. In 1972 Audemars Piguet introduced the Royal Oak, designed by Gérald Genta: a steel watch with a bracelet integrated into the case that cost as much as a gold one. Genta did it again in 1976 with Patek Philippe's Nautilus. Since then, "luxury sports watch" has meant steel, an integrated bracelet and dress-watch finishing.

## GADA

GADA stands for *go anywhere, do anything*; Spanish-speaking collectors call it CUCO, "any use, any occasion". It is the watch you put on without thinking: usually mid-sized, with reasonable water resistance, a bracelet and a legible dial. Many collectors start with one and end up with several.

## The other classification: case shape

- **Round:** the vast majority, partly because almost all movements are round.
- **Rectangular:** the classic example is the Cartier Tank of 1917. Usually a dress watch.
- **Square:** much less common.
- **Tonneau:** wide in the middle and narrower at the ends, like a barrel.
- **Cushion:** a square with bulging sides and rounded corners. It appears on some vintage divers and their reissues.

## Three practical rules

- **One family per watch, based on how you use it.** Many watches fit two: a diver with a GMT, or a pilot's chronograph. Choose by why you wear it, not by the spec sheet.
- **Shape doesn't decide the family.** There are round dress watches and rectangular sports watches.
- **Look for the gaps.** If all your watches are divers, what you're missing isn't another diver.
