On 25 December 1969, Seiko put the Quartz-Astron 35SQ on sale in Tokyo: the first quartz wristwatch. It cost 450,000 yen, roughly the price of a mid-sized car, and according to Seiko it drifted about ±5 seconds a month. A good mechanical watch of the day could drift that much in a single day.

That watch opened fifteen years that nearly finished off Swiss watchmaking. It is usually told as the story of one technology killing another, but what happened is more interesting.

## Switzerland was not late to quartz

At the 1970 Basel Fair the Swiss presented the **Beta 21**, a quartz calibre developed jointly by several brands and used by, among others, Omega in its Electroquartz. Omega kept going: in 1974 its Marine Chronometer became the first quartz watch certified as a marine chronometer, accurate to about 12 seconds a year.

In the lab, Switzerland was keeping pace. What it could not do was make cheap electronics at scale, which was exactly what quartz rewarded.

## Quartz alone did not sink Switzerland

Three factors tend to be forgotten:

- **The Swiss franc.** Under Bretton Woods the franc was fixed at about 4.37 to the dollar. After that system ended it rose to 2.5 in 1975 and reached 1.5 in October 1978. Abroad, a Swiss watch cost roughly three times more in 1978 than ten years earlier, with nothing about the watch having changed.
- **A highly fragmented industry.** Hundreds of small workshops in the Jura, protected for decades by rules that limited competition. That worked for making excellent mechanical watches, not for mass production.
- **The 1970s recession,** which cut what people could spend on a watch.

The result: Swiss watchmaking went from about 90,000 jobs in 1970 to just over 30,000 in 1984, and the number of companies fell from about 1,600 to about 600.

## The rescue came in plastic

In 1983 the two big struggling Swiss groups merged: ASUAG (which owned Longines, Mido and Oris, among others) and SSIH (Omega and Tissot). Soon after, Nicolas G. Hayek took control of the combined group, which would later be named the Swatch Group.

That same year the **Swatch** came out: a plastic quartz watch with 51 parts, for under 50 dollars. What gave Switzerland back its scale and its money was not an automatic, but a cheap, well-designed quartz watch.

## Those who bet on mechanics

With the volume business now handled by quartz, the mechanical watch had to find a new purpose. Two stories show how:

- **Oris.** In 1970 it became part of ASUAG, which pushed it into quartz, and in 1981 it stopped making its own movements. In 1982 two managers, Rolf Portmann and Ulrich W. Herzog, bought the company and decided to drop quartz and focus on mid-priced mechanical watches. Since 1992 it has made only mechanical watches, and since 2002 its red rotor has been a registered trademark.
- **Blancpain.** In 1983 Jean-Claude Biver and Jacques Piguet bought the name and relaunched it with a slogan that sounded almost provocative at the time: "Blancpain has never made a quartz watch and never will."

It was not simple in Japan either. Seiko, the brand that started it all, stopped making its mechanical Grand Seiko watches in 1975. The name returned in 1988 on a quartz watch, and there was no entirely new mechanical Grand Seiko movement until 1998.

## What wearing an automatic means today

For telling the time accurately, a 20-euro quartz watch beats almost any automatic. That argument was settled in 1969.

Wearing an automatic is something else. It means carrying a small engine that winds itself from the movement of your wrist, thanks to the **rotor**, the half-moon weight that spins inside the case. It is a technology that survived after it stopped being the most efficient one, because its role changed: from a tool to something you choose.

Rotor takes its name from that part. If your collection mixes automatics and quartz watches, the app reminds you which one has spent the longest in the box and tells you when a quartz watch is due for a battery check.
