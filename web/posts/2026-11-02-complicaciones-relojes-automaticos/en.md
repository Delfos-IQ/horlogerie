In watchmaking, a **complication** is anything a watch does beyond showing hours, minutes and seconds. A date is already a complication. So is a minute repeater that chimes the time.

In an automatic, every complication is one more mechanism running on the energy of the same mainspring that the **rotor** winds. That has practical consequences: more parts, a thicker watch, a more expensive service and, in some cases, more work when the watch stops in its box.

## At a glance

| Complication | What it does | What to know |
|---|---|---|
| Date | Shows the day of the month | Needs correcting after months shorter than 31 days |
| Day-date | Adds the day of the week | Like the date, with two discs to set |
| Annual calendar | Knows 30- and 31-day months | Corrected once a year, at the end of February |
| Perpetual calendar | Also knows February and leap years | No setting needed until 2100, as long as it keeps running |
| Moon phase | Shows today's moon | The common type drifts one day every 2 years and 7 months |
| Chronograph | Times events | Has more parts than any of the other common complications |
| GMT | Shows a second time zone | Not all GMTs work the same way |
| Power reserve | Shows how much wind is left | Very useful if you rotate several watches |

## Calendars

A **simple date** always counts to 31. Every time a 30-day month or February ends, you have to advance it by hand. Do it carefully: on many calibres you should not change the date while the hands are between roughly 9 pm and 3 am, because the date-change mechanism is already engaged. Move the hands to 6:30, set the date, then set the time.

Patek Philippe patented the **annual calendar** in 1996. It knows which months have 30 days and which have 31, so it only needs correcting once a year, from February to March.

A **perpetual calendar** also knows how many days each February has, leap years included. In theory it needs no setting until 2100, which will not be a leap year. In practice, if the watch stops, setting it means adjusting day, date, month and leap year, sometimes with recessed pushers you press with a stylus. This is the one case where a watch winder really makes sense.

## The moon phase

A lunar month lasts about 29.53 days. The most common moon phase uses a 59-tooth wheel that advances one tooth a day: two moons of 29.5 days. Those 44 minutes a month add up, and the display drifts one day roughly every 2 years, 7 months and 20 days. Precision versions with a 135-tooth wheel drift only one day every 122 years.

The real problem is not that error but setting it. After a week in the box, how many times do you press the corrector to bring the disc back in line with today's moon? You need to know when the last full moon was.

## The chronograph

A chronograph adds a mechanism that starts, stops and returns to zero with two pushers. It is one of the complications with the most parts and one of the most expensive to service.

The automatic chronograph's story ties in with [the quartz crisis](../the-quartz-crisis/): in 1969, the same year as the first quartz watch, Zenith presented its El Primero, a group made up of Heuer, Breitling, Hamilton-Buren and Dubois-Depraz presented its Calibre 11, and Seiko launched its 6139. Who came first is still debated.

A practical tip: unless the manufacturer says otherwise, don't use the pushers underwater.

## The GMT

A GMT shows a second time zone with a 24-hour hand. There are two types, and the difference matters if you travel:

- **"Traveller's" GMT:** the local hour hand jumps in one-hour steps without stopping the watch. When you land, you set local time in seconds and the date follows.
- **"Office" GMT:** the 24-hour hand is what moves independently. It is good for tracking another time zone from home, but when you travel you have to set the whole watch.

## The power reserve indicator

A hand or window showing how much wind the watch has left. It seems minor, but on an automatic it is very useful: it tells you whether the watch will make it to Monday or whether to give the crown a few turns before putting it away.

## The grand complications

At the top sit the **minute repeater**, which strikes the hours, quarters and minutes with tiny hammers on gongs, and the **tourbillon**, patented by Abraham-Louis Breguet in 1801. Strictly speaking, a tourbillon shows no new information: it rotates the regulating organ to offset gravity. But it is usually counted among complications because it is so hard to make.

## More complications, more care

Before buying a watch with complications, think about three things:

- **Servicing costs more.** Every added mechanism means more parts to strip, clean and adjust.
- **Stopping has a price.** A date takes a minute to set; a perpetual calendar with moon phase, a lot longer.
- **Rotation helps.** Wearing a watch regularly is the simplest way to keep it from stopping.
